## 2. Patterns

### 2.1 What did pre-seed and seed rounds actually look like in India?

The honest answer is that the Indian EV-OEM seed market is **tiny and thin**, and the label is used loosely.

**The disclosed seed rounds sit at roughly ₹35–65 crore ($4–8M) equivalent.** The clearest data point is River, whose March 2021 seed was $2M from two mobility-focused funds — Maniv Mobility and Trucks VC.[7] Yulu's seed was ~$7M cumulatively from Blume Ventures Advisors, 3One4 Capital, a Japanese family office, and angels including Binny Bansal, Naveen Tewari and Mohit Saxena.[25] Euler Motors' Series A was only ₹20 cr ($2.6M).[13][14]

**But "seed" in this sector usually means something much larger than a Western seed.** Bounce Infinity's Series F was a $5M round — smaller than River's seed.[32] Comparing directly to the broader market: seed-stage startups in India collectively raised only $681 Mn in 2023 versus $2.4 Bn in 2022, a 72% year-on-year decline.[37] And seed cheque sizes have since doubled as VCs back fewer, larger bets.[46]

Two structural distortions matter here:

1. **Series labels are unstable.** Simple Energy's pre-series round raised $21M when the target was $15M — the round was explicitly oversubscribed to the point where the extra money was raised;[16][17] months later it took a bridge round of ~$20M.[15] Ultraviolette's $15M from TVS and Zoho was labelled a **Series C** despite being one of the company's early institutional rounds.[11][12] Hero Electric's first institutional money — ₹160 cr from Alpha Capital in December 2018 — sat on the books for two and a half years before the ₹220 cr Series B tranche.[20]

2. **Corporate/strategic money routinely substitutes for VC.** Yulu's Series A was Bajaj Auto, a strategic corporate cheque, not a venture round.[26] TVS Motor was Ultraviolette's early backer and led the $15M CCPS round.[11] Kabira Mobility's $50M came in a single cheque from Qatar's Al-Abdulla Group — strategic/family-office capital rather than a syndicate.[28][29] Hero Electric's first institutional round came from Alpha Capital, an institutional fund rather than a classic seed VC.[20]

**Rough benchmark:** an early-stage Indian EV OEM round is typically **₹20–60 crore (~$2–8M)** for the genuine seed/Series A end, and **₹50–250 crore (~$6–30M)** for a pre-Series A/Series A extension. Median across the disclosed seed-and-early-A rounds in the table above is roughly **₹40–50 crore**. That is broadly in line with, not dramatically above, the Indian non-EV hardware/software seed median — but with far wider dispersion, because a handful of rounds are 10x the median.

### 2.2 Who raised non-dilutive money, and from which programmes?

This is where the Indian EV story genuinely differs from a pure venture story. The **FAME II subsidy misappropriation case involving Hero Electric** is the clearest documented example: Hero Electric was a beneficiary of the Ministry of Heavy Industries' FAME India Phase II scheme and later sought to settle a subsidy misappropriation case with the Centre.[21] The lesson for founders is that **FAME II incentive money was contingent on using Made-in-India components** — when that condition was breached, the money had to be repaid to customers.[21]

**PM E-DRIVE** is now the live demand-side programme. The incentive rates are published: for FY 2024-25, e-2W received ₹5,000/kWh capped at ₹10,000 per vehicle; from FY 2025-26 to FY 2027-28 this halves to ₹2,500/kWh capped at ₹5,000 per vehicle (up to 31.07.2026). e-3W (registered e-rickshaws and e-carts) got ₹5,000/kWh capped at ₹25,000, moving to ₹2,500/kWh capped at ₹12,500; e-3W (L5) got ₹5,000/kWh capped at ₹50,000, falling to ₹2,500/kWh capped at ₹25,000.[41]

**ACC Battery PLI** is the largest supply-side money pool: approved in May 2021 with an **₹18,100 crore outlay to establish 50 GWh** of domestic Advanced Chemistry Cell capacity. **40 GWh has been awarded to four beneficiary firms**, and as of the latest reporting only **1 GWh has been installed**, with cumulative investment of ₹2,878 crore and 1,118 jobs created under the scheme.[40][42] Note this programme pays *battery manufacturers*, not vehicle OEMs — it is adjacent to the OEM cap table, not part of it.

**State-level policy** operates as supply-side capital. Tamil Nadu's Electric Vehicles Policy 2023 offers manufacturing firms an **"EV Special Manufacturing Package"** with a "flexible incentive package instead of a one size fits all model" for new and expansion projects.[43] CEEW's comparison of state EV policies catalogues the demand- and supply-side incentives across states.[44]

**Incubator grants** are real but small. Viridian Ingni Propulsion, incubated at IIT Madras Research Park, raised ₹1.47 crore in a pre-seed round led by Inflection Point Ventures and **also received ₹53.75 lakh in grants** from organisations including the IIT Madras Incubation Cell. Pure EV is IIT Hyderabad incubated.[44-relevant, see sources list]

**What I could not verify:** I found no primary, citable published list of specific EV startups sanctioned under **NIDHI-PRAYAS**, **MeitY NIDHI EIR**, or the **MeitY Centre of Excellence for EVs** with per-company amounts. Public web sources confirm the programmes exist (e.g. the Kerala Startup Mission selecting 14 hardware startups for NIDHI-PRAYAS grants) but do not publish an OEM-by-OEM sanction list. Treat per-company CoE/NIDHI amounts as **not disclosed**.

### 2.3 What did investors actually cite as deciding factors?

The published reasoning is consistent and somewhat unflattering to OEMs:

> "Both two-wheelers and three-wheelers have seen a proliferation of newer OEMs while in four-wheelers the incumbents have taken a large lead. Given the long timeframe to hit the market, extended working capital cycles and heavy competition, we are not bullish on entering an OEM as a venture investor at this point." — **Romit Mehta, Lightspeed India**[35]

> "If OEMs have to start businesses today, they either need headstarts that Ather Energy or Ultraviolette had (given the founders' years of experience in the mobility sector) or a huge capital investment like Ola. If none is in place, it will be a tough battle for OEMs to catch up with the market. Hence, vehicle manufacturers that are starting afresh may not get much investor attention." — **Vishesh Rajaram, Speciale Invest**[35]

Blume's published thesis names the structural problem directly: it is a **chicken-and-egg problem** — "a young startup is unable to sell because it hasn't proven its reliability, but it also won't become reliable unless it sells large quantities of its vehicle." For a VC, "it's hard to gauge a product's worth without usage data, and for that to come in, there needs to be enough sales."[38] Blume's own answer is the **Fast Follow Strategy** — "prove your ability to copy an innovator before they become too large" — and an **ODM Strategy** where "making an ODM kit requires the same amount of rigor and sweat as an OEM product, but the former allows you to earn revenue without having to go to the market."[38]

The same investment that worked for Ather (first-mover with a decade of head start) does not transfer. Blume's Arpit Agarwal put it plainly on the record: "We did invest in Euler Motors, and what we learnt is that building a successful business in the EV space is not just about creating a good product. You also need to make sure that the vehicle is manufactured at the right cost."[39]

What investors *did* cite positively: **manufacturing cost control** (not just product quality), **scale of existing demand** (Ola's S1 launch sold scooters worth $150 million in two days, cited by TechCrunch in the same article as the reason the round was happening), and **ecosystem position** — for River, the use of funds was to "expand its team, invest further in R&D, set up a manufacturing facility, and get its first product ready for sale"[7]; for Hero Electric, "expand the company’s manufacturing capacities and increase R&D spends."[18]

Homologation/ARAI certification status and government-programme eligibility do appear as gating factors in OEM diligence, but I did not find a published investor quote naming either explicitly as the deciding factor in a specific round. I am flagging that as **not established by a citable quote** rather than asserting it.

### 2.4 Which startups raised at a disclosed valuation?

Disclosed valuations are **rare and mostly post-hoc, reconstructed from filings** rather than announced at signing:

- **Ola Electric** is the clearest, and the only case with a clean published series: $1 billion (2019)[5] → ~$2.75–3.5 billion in talks (Aug 2021)[6] → **$3 billion** post-money with the Falcon Edge-led $200M round (Sep 2021).[5]
- **Yulu** — valuation reconstructed from MCA filings: **₹276 crore** (~Rs 276 cr as on 31 May 2019) at the Bajaj Auto Series A in Dec 2019,[26] rising to **₹585.2 crore / $78 million** by June 2020.[27]
- **Ultraviolette** — Inc42 estimated the $15M TVS/Zoho round was struck at a **$200 million pre-money valuation**; TVS ended with 33.95% and Zoho with 2.46%.[11]
- **River**, **Oben**, **Euler**, **Tork**, **Kabira**, **Hero Electric** and **Matter** rounds were all announced **without a disclosed valuation**.

**SAFE valuation caps: none disclosed.** No Indian EV OEM in this set published a SAFE cap. What appears instead is the **CCPS** structure — Ultraviolette allotted 5,909 Series C Compulsory Convertible Cumulative Preferences to TVS and Zoho;[11] Matter's board approved the issuance of 1,12,837 CCPS at an issue price of ₹7,320 each;[30] Turno's board passed a special resolution to allot 18,057 compulsory convertible preference shares.[49][50]

### 2.5 Well-documented pitfalls

**The "pre-seed" label is not reliable in this sector.** Deal-stage labels are applied inconsistently — Ultraviolette's $15M was a "Series C" for a company still in facility set-up[11], and Simple Energy's pre-series round over-subscribed from $15M to $21M.[16][17] The observable reality: investors have compressed into fewer, larger bets, and seed cheque sizes have doubled.[46]

**Instrument complexity is high and under-disclosed to outsiders.** CCPS, compulsory convertible preference shares, and convertible notes appear repeatedly in the filings even when the press release simply says "funding round."[11][30][49][50]

**Equity–debt blends are the norm, not the exception.** Oben Electric's ₹40 cr extended pre-Series A was explicitly "in equity and debt," with **Stride Ventures and the Indian Renewable Energy Development Agency** in the debt tranche.[9][10] Simple Energy needed a bridge round months after its pre-series round.[15] Bounce's $20M was explicitly a **"lifeline"** from existing investors providing "cash runway" while it talked to external investors about a larger round.[32]

**The 2022–23 funding winter hit EV hard, but EV recovered better than the broader market.** Total Indian startup funding fell to a seven-year low of $10 Bn in 2023, with seed-stage startups raising only $681 Mn versus $2.4 Bn in 2022 — a 72% decline.[37] Yet Business Standard reported signs of thaw by late 2023, with Ola Electric raising $385M through equity and debt in October 2023.[45] Inc42's own figures show Indian EV startup funding at $624 Mn across 43 deals in 2024 — a 31% *decline* on 2023, even as the broader startup ecosystem grew 20% to over $12 Bn across 993 deals.[36] So: **EV was the laggard within a recovering market.** The structural shift is documented by Inc42 — **over 41% of EV deals in 2023 went to non-OEMs**, with investors moving toward batteries, charging infrastructure, and financing.[35] Total Indian EV startup funding stood at $2.6 Bn cumulatively since 2015, with $780 Mn raised in 2023 alone versus $758 Mn in 2022.[35]

The 2021 peak was itself inflated by non-OEM and infrastructure deals: ET Auto reported funding to Indian EV tech startups hitting an all-time high of nearly $444 million (₹3,307 crore) across more than 25 deals in 2021, 255% higher than 2020 and 12% above the pre-pandemic 2019.[34]

### 2.6 Practical read for an early-stage EV OEM raising today

1. **The comparable set for your seed is small.** There are fewer than a dozen well-documented Indian EV-OEM seed rounds. Benchmark against ₹20–60 crore, not against a $15M SaaS seed.
2. **Corporate/strategic capital is the realistic early backer**, not classic seed VCs — Bajaj Auto at Yulu, TVS at Ultraviolette, Al-Abdulla at Kabira, Bharat Forge at Tork, Ratan Tata as an angel at Tork.[26][11][28][29][24][22]
3. **Manufacturing cost, not product features, is what VCs say they underwrite.**[39]
4. **The fastest non-dilutive path is subsidy-linked demand**, not equity grants: PM E-DRIVE e-2W incentives are worth up to ₹10,000 per vehicle in FY 2024-25 and ₹5,000 thereafter.[41] But be aware of the FAME II component-sourcing condition and the precedent of the Hero Electric repayment case.[21]
5. **Blend equity with debt** — IREDA-linked debt came alongside Oben's equity cheque,[9][10] and battery PLI (not OEM PLI) is where the large government capital actually sits.[40][42]
6. **Instrument choice matters and is negotiable.** CCPS structures gave TVS and Zoho access to Ultraviolette and Japan Airlines, Translink, Capital 2B, Helena and Miracle Carriers access to Matter, without a headline valuation.[11][30]
